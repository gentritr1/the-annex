# UI Patterns for The Annex

## The core answer

The strongest lesson from narrative investigation games is not “hide the text.” It is **separate kinds of reading, and make each one belong somewhere**. The games that balance literary density with atmosphere usually do three things at once: they keep the *live scene* visually dominant; they move dense reading into a clearly framed *document mode* or *archive mode*; and they let systems become legible through props, rituals, typography, pacing devices, and recurring metaphors instead of naked labels. That is why *Return of the Obra Dinn* turns investigation into a book, *Papers, Please* turns state into a desk and rulebook, *Pentiment* turns social information into script and ink, *Her Story* turns search into thought, and *Citizen Sleeper* turns pressure into clocks, drives, and up-front dice rather than hidden math. The result is not less text, but text that arrives in the right vessel. citeturn24view2turn24view3turn24view1turn26view3turn26view0turn25view3

For **The Annex**, that implies a UI strategy built on a hybrid of **spatial scene interaction**, **meta-representational documents**, and **signifiers** rather than an all-or-nothing commitment to pure diegesis. Fagerholt and Lorentzon’s UI taxonomy remains useful here: non-diegetic overlays are best for highly detailed information, meta-representations are fictional artifacts shown outside world geometry, and signifiers are subtle cues the player interprets rather than reads as explicit numbers or labels. Their work also argues that non-diegetic and meta-perceptual UI do **not automatically** break immersion, which matters because The Annex should not martyr clarity for atmosphere. citeturn21view0turn23view1turn23view2

So the short conclusion is this: **The Annex should keep the room in charge, but give text a ritual place to land**. Use scene-first subtitle beats for live conversation; use dossier, ledger, deposition, and seal metaphors for close reading; use recurring atmospheric cues, bureaucratic artifacts, and portrait/body-language changes to explain trust, stance, and reconstruction; and reserve explicit language for confirmations, accessibility, and archival views. That is the pattern used, in different forms, by the best-in-class exemplars in this space. citeturn22view1turn21view3turn19search10turn20search2turn25view2turn14search0

## What the exemplar games actually solve

*Disco Elysium* shows how to move abstract systems into fiction by giving the detective’s skills distinct “voices” that interrupt thought and dialogue. That works because the mechanics are not explained as UI chrome first; they arrive as characterful interior arguments. ZA/UM’s official description emphasizes that the game’s 24 skills have distinct personalities that talk to the protagonist and unlock different solutions, which turns comprehension into dramaturgy rather than documentation. For The Annex, that is the single best precedent for replacing blunt stance labels with internally voiced legal, ethical, or procedural “committees.” citeturn24view0

*Citizen Sleeper* solves density differently. Its prose is substantial, but the game keeps orientation through **up-front dice, clocks, drives, and strongly illustrated cast anchors**. Gareth Damian Martin explicitly frames the design as an attempt to bring lightweight tabletop ideas into videogames, and both official and interview material stress that dice are rolled in advance so the player manages visible possibility rather than opaque randomness. It is also notable that Martin calls out UI/localization pressure from a prose-heavy interface, which is directly relevant to a browser literary game. The transferable lesson is to expose pressure through *fictionalized structures* and *strong character anchoring*, not status words floating over the world. citeturn25view3turn26view0turn26view1

*Pentiment* is the clearest model for **comprehension-through-style**. Its lettering is not decorative frosting; script and writing effects carry social, historical, and even investigative information. Obsidian’s accessibility page explicitly says the game uses stylized fonts and writing effects that can be difficult for some readers, hence the Easy-read option that disables some of them. Contemporary coverage also noted that font changes can signal character background and even deception, while remaining redundant because the game provides an easier reading mode. The Annex can borrow this directly: handwriting, type pressure, stamp wear, and correction marks can communicate reliability, class position, institutional rank, or memory integrity without adding explanatory labels. citeturn24view1turn12search12turn4search5

*Return of the Obra Dinn* and *The Case of the Golden Idol* are the best paired investigation references. Lucas Pope says the Obra Dinn book was meant to “collect and organize the deluge of information,” establish chronology, and provide a “story-like book” as the most implicitly useful metaphor. Color Gray’s designers, meanwhile, describe *Golden Idol* as a response to a lack of games that make the player feel like a detective through gameplay rather than flavor, and they emphasize trust in the player’s reasoning plus extensive testing to preserve the “aha” moment. Together, these two games show the winning split: **free observation first, structured report second**. That is ideal for The Annex’s casework, because bureaucratic-noir fiction naturally supports certificates, deposits, summaries, exhibits, and adjudication forms. citeturn24view2turn20search2turn25view2

*Papers, Please* is the best model for turning bureaucracy into embodied play. Pope has said one design mantra was to “keep it vague,” so that players felt thrown into a job with no training, but beta complaints pushed him to centralize crucial information in the rulebook. Earlier in development he also insisted on interface consistency, even making strip-searches appear as photos so they behaved like any other document. This is relevant to The Annex in two opposing ways: first, it proves that bureaucratic procedures can become tactile verbs; second, it warns that fiction-first opacity eventually needs a central, legible policy surface. citeturn24view3turn25view0

*Kentuky Route Zero*, *NORCO*, *Her Story*, *Immortality*, *Signalis*, and *Roadwarden* fill in the rest of the pattern field. Cardboard Computer’s GDC framing for *Kentucky Route Zero* explicitly ties the game to theatrical stagecraft, scenography, and slow cinema; that is your best precedent for scene-first composition and quiet text staging. Polygon highlighted *NORCO*’s mind-map as a pacing and legibility tool that keeps a complex story relatively easy to follow. Sam Barlow’s official line on *Her Story*—“If you can Google, you can play”—captures the power of a simple query metaphor whose richness emerges from player curiosity, while his *Immortality* remarks on “friction” show how controlled inconvenience can support narrative mood. *Signalis* adds the lesson of analog object scrutiny and retrotech tactility. *Roadwarden* shows how a text-heavy game can still maintain place identity with a large map, isometric art, and an attitude system that changes the mood and direction of conversation rather than just displaying affinity bars. citeturn27view1turn28view2turn14search0turn26view3turn26view2turn27view0turn16search2turn15search2turn28view0

## What research and accessibility guidance imply for The Annex

For Annex-specific UI architecture, the most useful theoretical lens is still the **diegetic / non-diegetic / meta-representational / signifier** split. Fagerholt and Lorentzon argue that conventional HUDs sit in the non-fictional, non-spatial quadrant; meta-representations are fictional things presented outside world geometry; diegetic UI exists both in the fiction and the world; and signifiers are subtle cues that ask the player to infer rather than simply read. They also note that non-diegetic overlays are especially suitable for highly detailed information, while signifiers are better for subtle logical interpretation. That maps almost perfectly onto The Annex: the painted room should carry signifiers and spatial choices; the dossier and case file should act as meta-representations; and only confirmation, accessibility, and archival depth should become plainly non-diegetic. citeturn21view0turn23view1turn23view2

For the text-over-world problem, game subtitle guidance is surprisingly concrete. Microsoft’s Xbox Accessibility Guideline 104 recommends mixed-case text, at least one sans-serif option, manual line breaks, generally no more than two subtitle lines at once, configurable backgrounds and opacity, speaker indication, and avoidance of long lines—especially during fast dialogue. It also recommends a preview of subtitle presentation. Guideline 101 adds minimum text-size recommendations and calls out scaling to 200% without loss of content or meaning. Those are not just accessibility notes; they are production rules for keeping scene text from burying the image. citeturn22view1turn22view2

Subtitle research supports the same direction. Work on segmentation finds that readability suffers when line breaks cut across linguistic phrases, and the broader literature argues that subtitle presentation must facilitate reading because viewers are already splitting attention across image, sound, and text. A 2026 eye-tracking study on video games reiterates that in-game text directly affects action, progression, and experience, and that subtitles differ from other text because they arrive under changing task demand and with limited repetition. For The Annex, this means the live scene should use *short, manually broken subtitle-like beats*, while the full deposition, affidavit, or archival paragraph belongs in a separate reading mode. citeturn21view1turn21view2

Visual-novel practice gives a useful operational split. Ren’Py’s documentation defines **ADV-style** presentation as dialogue and narration one line at a time in a lower window, and **NVL-style** as multiple lines at once in a screen-filling text window. That distinction is directly usable for The Annex. Your current staged, subtitle-like dialogue is essentially an atmospheric ADV mode. What you need is a controlled NVL counterpart for records, testimony, reconstructed memory packets, or legal findings. This is a much better answer than forcing every kind of text into the same scene-first overlay. citeturn21view3

Dark-mode and low-light readability research also gives a caution. NN/g notes that dark interfaces fail when fonts are too thin, too thick, or too low-contrast; that depth in dark mode often requires *lighter raised surfaces over darker grounds* rather than simply making everything black; and that thin dividers and weak cards disappear in dark conditions. Combined with WCAG contrast requirements and game accessibility guidance, the implication is simple: if The Annex lives in rain-dark interiors, it should avoid very thin literary faces for primary reading, use local plates or distinct luminance steps behind text, and allow contrast / size adjustments. Atmosphere is not harmed by this; amateur dark-mode styling is. citeturn22view4turn7search3turn7search19

## Pattern catalog mapped to The Annex

**District concourse — scenographic routing plus civic signifiers.** Borrow *Kentucky Route Zero*’s scenic blocking and “slow cinema” logic to make the concourse legible by composition before language: lamplight pools, shelter eaves, queue direction, clerical silhouettes, and moving rain sheets should imply where attention belongs. Then use *Roadwarden*-style place identity and *Citizen Sleeper*-style pressure structures, but rephrase them as civic artifacts: posted hearing slips, stamped transit notices, wet registry cards, and wall-borne amendments rather than explicit system labels. In DOM/CSS, this is straightforward: layered painted backgrounds, absolute-position ring zones, subtle parallax from transforms, weather masks, and summoned side-cards. Accessibility note: every spatial hotspot needs keyboard focus, screen-reader labels, and an alternate list in the Case File for users who cannot rely on image hunting. citeturn27view1turn28view2turn28view0turn26view0

**Close-read ritual rooms — free observation first, structured filing second.** This surface should be the clearest fusion of *Obra Dinn* and *Golden Idol*: let the player inspect the room, exhibits, seals, hands, stains, tags, and memory residue freely, then ask them to complete a narrowly structured filing action—a certificate, excerpted summary, exhibit chain, or reconstruction clause. That keeps discovery in the room but moves commitment into a formal document. In DOM/CSS, the “book” or “folio” can be a summon layer with page states, fillable blanks, and lock-in validation on grouped propositions rather than constant correctness feedback. Accessibility note: every visual clue should have a later textual echo in logs, dossiers, or alt descriptions, because these games are powerful precisely when clues are duplicated across channels, not trapped in pure image reading. citeturn24view2turn20search2turn25view2

**Deposition and consent beat — subtitle staging for the room, NVL for the record.** Keep live dialogue as short staged beats over the scene, with strong manual line breaks, no more than two lines at once, optional speaker labeling, and a configurable local plate behind the text. Then, when the scene transitions into formal deposition or consent review, shift into a denser “record view” modeled on NVL: a page, transcript strip, or thin legal folio that acknowledges reading as reading. This prevents the current problem where all text competes with the room in the same visual register. In DOM/CSS, you can do this with one shared text system that swaps classes and containers between scene mode and record mode. Accessibility note: the scene mode must support larger text and background opacity options; the record mode must support font scaling, reflow, and an Easy Read variant. citeturn22view1turn22view2turn21view3

**Deposition and consent beat — replace stance labels with voiced or materialized legal weather.** *Disco Elysium* suggests a high-payoff move here: instead of showing “uncertain / open / committed,” let a small internal chorus—perhaps Clerk, Ghost, Statute, Mercy, or Precedent—briefly color a line or a hover. If you prefer less literal personification, express the same state through Pentiment-like document styling: wavering baseline, overcorrections, seal pressure, margin crowding, second-ink insertions, or rain response on hover. DOM/CSS feasibility is high because all of this is typography, layering, timing, and micro-animation. Accessibility note: these signals must remain redundant. If a player enables Easy Read, the system should replace stylistic cues with plainer descriptors in a tooltip, glossary, or toggleable notation layer. citeturn24view0turn24view1turn12search12

**Tribunal verdict — make judgment a desk ritual, not a modal popup.** *Papers, Please* proves that bureaucracy becomes memorable when the player physically enacts it. For The Annex, verdict delivery should feel like assembling and ratifying a living file: align exhibits, place a seal, countersign a restoration status, slide a page under glass, or unlock a registry ribbon. The model is not “minigameification”; it is procedural gravity. In DOM/CSS, drag/drop and confirm affordances, layered papers, animated stamps, and constrained placement are enough. Accessibility note: every drag action must also be completable via keyboard buttons or a simplified “form mode,” because tactile ritual should be optional, not mandatory. citeturn25view0turn24view3turn28view3

**Debrief — externalize comprehension as a query trail and belief map.** *NORCO*’s mind-map and *Her Story*’s query metaphor suggest that post-scene debrief should not just summarize outcomes; it should show how the tribunal—or the player—came to its current belief. The right object for The Annex is not a lore dump but a **belief chain**: queried names, linked records, contradictory deposits, recovered minutes, and “fourth minute” anomalies. In DOM/CSS, this can be a zoomable but quiet network inside the Case File, with timeline pins and search trails. Accessibility note: every node network needs an equivalent linear outline view, plus filters for “new this scene,” “contested,” and “confirmed.” citeturn14search0turn26view3

**The Case-file codex — turn the codex into the game’s legal memory, not its encyclopedia.** The best precedent here is *Obra Dinn*’s book: a central organizer that establishes chronology, stores clues, and acts as the investigation’s story-like metaphor. The Case File in The Annex should work the same way. It should not explain the world at leisure while the room does the “real game.” It should be where chronology, deposition chains, personhood precedent, and restoration history become thinkable. Borrow *Her Story*’s searchability and *Immortality*’s respect for curiosity-driven browsing: let the player search terms, names, docket numbers, and phrases, and keep the query history as part of the fiction. Accessibility note: this surface is where full transcripts, glossary expansions, image descriptions, and reduced-motion alternatives should live. citeturn24view2turn26view3turn27view0

**The persona dossier — use portraiture plus marginalia instead of stats.** Here *Citizen Sleeper* and *Pentiment* are the best fit. Sleeper’s cast art gives individuals an immediate face-value anchor; Pentiment turns textual ornament into social information. The Annex’s dossiers should therefore feel like living case folders: portrait plate, affiliation seal, known inconsistencies, body-language notes, prior testimony snippets, handwriting samples, and the tribunal’s changing annotations in the margins. That is how you surface trust or reconstruction state without affinity bars. In DOM/CSS, this is a card stack, margin notes, stamped states, and progressive disclosure. Accessibility note: portrait changes should never be sole carriers of meaning; their significance must be captured in accessible alt text or dossier notes. citeturn26view0turn24view1turn4search5

## Failure modes and the guardrails worth keeping

The first failure mode is **codex dumping**. *Papers, Please* is instructive because Pope wanted players to feel their way through a hostile ruleset, but eventually had to centralize scattered information in the rulebook when players could not orient themselves. The lesson is not “avoid codices”; it is “make the codex the right kind of object.” If The Annex’s Case File starts doing all the explanatory labor that the scenes, portraits, deposits, and artifacts should be doing, the world will flatten into homework. citeturn24view3

The second failure mode is **diegesis so pure it hides the game**. Fagerholt and Lorentzon explicitly note that non-diegetic elements are especially good at carrying a high level of detail and that overlay elements do not necessarily destroy the game experience. That matters because The Annex is literary, not ascetic. If a deposition needs a clear plate, a contradiction needs a crisp highlight, or a search result needs a clean list, those are not betrayals of tone. They are the conditions under which tone survives contact with use. citeturn21view0turn23view2

The third failure mode is **tooltip dependence and label debt**. If trust, stance, memory reliability, or reconstruction quality are legible only when a user hovers a label, then the fiction is not carrying the system. Better games shift meaning into repeatable structures: document wear, typography, clocks, search trails, queue rituals, group validations, and mental maps. Labels should confirm an inference, not be the inference. *Golden Idol*, *Obra Dinn*, and *Her Story* all succeed because the player’s thought process is the main event. citeturn25view2turn24view2turn26view3

The fourth failure mode is **stylized text without an accessibility escape hatch**. *Pentiment* explicitly ships an Easy-read option because its manuscript styling can hinder legibility; Microsoft’s subtitle and text guidelines similarly require scalable text, configurable backgrounds, and careful line length; and game accessibility guidance stresses customization, speaker indication, and captions before first audio. The Annex’s literary ambitions only strengthen the case for an Easy Read mode, transcript log, and robust caption controls. citeturn24view1turn12search12turn22view1turn22view2turn22view3

The fifth failure mode is **a beautiful dark interface that reads badly**. NN/g’s dark-mode guidance is blunt: thin fonts get swallowed, thick fonts bleed, weak contrast disappears, and layered surfaces in dark interfaces need luminance separation. If The Annex leans into rain-black bureaucratic noir, it should do so with deliberate value steps, not fashionable murk. Local text plates, slightly lighter reading surfaces, and stable contrast will preserve atmosphere better than translucent cleverness that fails under real play conditions. citeturn22view4turn7search26

## The highest-payoff enrichments

- **Split the text system into Scene Mode and Record Mode.**  
  **What it does:** Scene Mode keeps conversations as subtitle-like beats over the painting; Record Mode handles depositions, affidavits, archived memories, and legal findings in a denser folio/NVL presentation.  
  **Why it pays off:** This is the cleanest answer to text dominance because it stops asking one text container to do two incompatible jobs.  
  **Effort:** **Medium**.  
  **Addresses:** **text-dominance, atmosphere, accessibility**. citeturn22view1turn21view3turn21view2

- **Replace stance words with a diegetic “legal weather” package.**  
  **What it does:** Trust and stance surface through rain tone, seal pressure, file neatness, portrait posture, handwriting stability, and short internal legal/ethical interjections.  
  **Why it pays off:** It directly attacks the current reliance on labels while deepening civic-noir tone.  
  **Effort:** **Medium**.  
  **Addresses:** **comprehension, atmosphere**. citeturn24view0turn24view1

- **Build an Obra Dinn-style ledger with Golden Idol-style structured filings.**  
  **What it does:** Free scene reading flows into grouped formal entries—exhibit chains, findings, identity claims, cause statements, or personhood grounds.  
  **Why it pays off:** It preserves detective pleasure while giving players a strong bureaucratic metaphor for understanding systems.  
  **Effort:** **Medium**.  
  **Addresses:** **comprehension, text-dominance**. citeturn24view2turn25view2

- **Turn the tribunal verdict into a tactile paper ritual.**  
  **What it does:** Align, seal, countersign, and file rather than clicking a generic confirm button.  
  **Why it pays off:** The fiction of registries, records, and civic adjudication becomes the mechanic itself.  
  **Effort:** **Medium to Large**.  
  **Addresses:** **atmosphere, comprehension**. citeturn25view0turn24view3

- **Give the Case File a searchable query trail.**  
  **What it does:** Search terms, docket IDs, person names, and report excerpts become the navigation spine, with history preserved as a thinking trace.  
  **Why it pays off:** It transforms archival reading from passive codex browsing into active investigation.  
  **Effort:** **Medium**.  
  **Addresses:** **comprehension, text-dominance**. citeturn26view3turn27view0

- **Add Pentiment-style document semantics plus an Easy Read toggle.**  
  **What it does:** Use script variation, corrections, counter-signatures, stamps, and ink behavior to convey social and evidentiary meaning, with an option to flatten those styles for readability.  
  **Why it pays off:** It enriches fiction while staying safe for accessibility and localization.  
  **Effort:** **Small to Medium**.  
  **Addresses:** **atmosphere, comprehension, accessibility**. citeturn24view1turn12search12turn4search5

- **Make the debrief a belief map instead of a prose dump.**  
  **What it does:** Show the chain of evidence, contradictions, and unresolved anomalies through a mind-map / timeline hybrid.  
  **Why it pays off:** The player leaves each scene with a memory of structure, not just a wall of recap text.  
  **Effort:** **Medium**.  
  **Addresses:** **comprehension**. citeturn14search0turn26view3

- **Lock in subtitle-grade presentation controls now.**  
  **What it does:** Manual line breaks, no more than two lines in live scene mode, configurable background opacity, scalable size, mixed case, speaker indication, and a visual preview.  
  **Why it pays off:** It is the cheapest high-return fix for readability without surrendering atmosphere.  
  **Effort:** **Small**.  
  **Addresses:** **text-dominance, accessibility**. citeturn22view1turn22view2turn22view3

The pattern that emerges across all of these is consistent: the best games in this lineage do not solve literary density by eliminating prose. They solve it by **reassigning prose to containers, giving systems a dramatic form, and letting the world—not the HUD—do more explanatory work**. For a browser-based, DOM/CSS-only game, that is good news. Nearly all of the highest-value techniques in this genre are really about **composition, layering, typography, pacing, and document metaphors**, which are exactly the domains where the web stack is strongest. citeturn24view2turn24view3turn24view1turn26view3turn27view1
